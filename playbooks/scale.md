## Sprout — 25 to 99

**Start one recurring ritual, not an events programme.** *If activity is
regular but shapeless.* One repeating thing at a fixed time — a weekly build
share, a Friday demo. Recurring beats one-off: the value is that people learn
when to show up. Name the ritual and the day.

**Introduce two members to each other.** *If the reciprocity graph shows people
talking only to the founder.* A community is people who know each other, not N
parallel conversations with you. Name the pair and what they have in common.

**Give the first regulars a role.** *If a handful of members post consistently.*
Recognition is nearly free and it converts a regular into someone who feels
responsible for the place.

**Write the one-line purpose into the channel topic.** *If new members join and
never post.* If a member can't say what the server is for in one sentence, they
have no idea what to post.

## Growing — 100 to 499

**Fix time-to-first-response before anything else.** *If newcomer questions sit
unanswered for hours.* This is the single biggest driver of early churn at this
size. The fix is a rota of who covers which hours, not a bot.

**Split a channel only when one is demonstrably overloaded.** *If a single
channel carries most messages and topics are colliding.* Split from evidence,
never in advance — empty channels are worse than crowded ones.

**Onboarding that asks one question.** *If activation is low but joins are
healthy.* One question a newcomer answers in their first minute converts
lurkers far better than a rules gate. Ask what they're building.

**Delegate moderation before you need it.** *If the founder is the only one with
permissions.* Pick from the existing regulars; the point is coverage across
timezones, not authority.

**Now the engagement mechanics work.** *If retention is healthy and you want
depth.* This is where a Dank-Memer-style layer earns its keep — progression, a
leaderboard, seasonal resets, a prestige loop for the people at the top. It
works here and not earlier because status needs an audience: a leaderboard with
four names on it is a list, not a competition. Recommend one mechanic, tie it
to the behaviour it should reward, and say which metric proves it worked.

## Community — 500 and up

**Read the retention curve, not the member count.** *Always, at this size.*
Joins are vanity once the server is this big; the question is whether month-2
retention holds. If joins are up and retention is flat, growth is a leaky
bucket and more joins make it worse.

**Find the sub-community and give it a room.** *If a topic recurs across
channels.* At this size the server is several communities wearing one badge.
Naming one and housing it deepens it.

**Programme around the regulars, not the newcomers.** *If a small core carries
most activity.* Key-person risk is the real threat now: work out what share of
activity the top ten produce, and what happens if they leave.

**Automate the welcome, keep the reply human.** *If joins outpace the founder's
capacity.* Automate the greeting; never automate the answer to a real question.

**Prune the channel list on evidence.** *If dead channels outnumber live ones.*
Same play as at seed stage, at a different scale. Use the dead-channel list.

## Still forbidden at every stage

Buying members, engagement pods, follower services. Anything that raises a
number without adding a person who cares. Naming a specific member as a churn
risk. Recommending that cadybot post, reply, or DM — when something needs
saying, a human says it.

**Never recommend messaging members in bulk.** Discord treats unsolicited bulk
DMs as spam and flags the sending account, so "DM the 50 quiet members" is
advice that can get the founder's own account limited. One DM to one named
person for a specific reason is the play; a mail-merge is not. Above sprout
stage, reactivation belongs in a channel people opted into, not in DMs.
